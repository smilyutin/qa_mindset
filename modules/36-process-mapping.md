**Make the Workflow Visible, Understand the Experience**

---

## Definition

Process mapping shows the steps, decisions, responsibilities, and handoffs in a workflow.

A useful map captures how work actually happens, including waiting, rework and exceptions. It gives staff and service users a shared view of the journey before changes are proposed.

---

## Relevant Example

> “I would map a request from initial contact through assessment, decision, and communication. A swimlane map would show who owns each step and where requests wait or move between teams.”

An illustrative workflow might look like this:

| Step | Responsible person or team | Decision or handoff to explore |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Initial contact | Individual or family and intake staff | Is the request understood and recorded? |
| Completeness check | Intake staff | Is more information needed? Who explains what is missing? |
| Assessment | Assessment team | Is the information sufficient to assess the request? |
| Decision | Responsible decision-maker | Is further review needed before communicating the decision? |
| Communication | Named contact | Does the person understand the decision and available next steps? |

Treat these roles as examples to validate locally. In a swimlane map, place activities in the lane of the person or team doing them and draw connections across lanes to expose handoffs. Show missing-information loops, queues and alternative paths.

---

## Mental Model 1: Map the Current Process First

Ask people to walk through a real request. Capture what happened rather than only what the procedure says should happen.

Agree where the map starts and ends. A request marked complete internally may still leave the person waiting for an explanation or support.

---

## Mental Model 2: Handoffs Need Ownership

For each transfer, identify who sends the request, who receives it, what information moves and how receipt is confirmed.

A handoff without clear ownership can become a queue that nobody sees.

---

## Mental Model 3: Waiting Is Part of the Journey

Separate time spent working on a request from time it spends waiting. Ask what triggers the next step and what happens when the expected response does not arrive.

---

## Mental Model 4: Exceptions Reveal Gaps

Include incomplete requests, additional support needs, reassessments and difficult experiences. A map of the happy path alone can hide the places where people need the most help.

---

## Measures

| Measure | What to record |
| --- | --- |
| Number of handoffs | Transfers of responsibility between people or teams for each request. |
| Waiting time between steps | Time from one step finishing to the next step starting. |
| Repeated tasks | Checks, data entry or requests for information repeated within the same journey. |
| End-to-end completion time | Elapsed time between the agreed start and end of the workflow. |

Use consistent definitions and examine differences between straightforward and complex requests. Validate timings with records and participant accounts where available.

---

## My Experience

> “My QA experience includes tracing user journeys, business rules, integrations, and data exchanges to identify gaps. I would describe specific formal mapping techniques or deliverables only where I have actually used them.”

These skills help identify missing paths, unclear responsibilities and broken handoffs. Keep the distinction clear between tracing a workflow during QA and producing a formal process map or swimlane deliverable.

---

## Service-User Involvement and Unintended Effects

> “I would validate the map with both staff and service users, including exceptions and difficult experiences. Before removing a step, I would check whether it provides an important safeguard, explanation, or opportunity for support.”

Ask staff whether the map reflects daily work. Ask individuals and families where they repeat information, wait without updates or struggle to understand what happens next.

Before changing the workflow, check:

- Whether a step protects privacy, checks accuracy or supports a fair decision.
- Whether it provides an explanation, accessible communication or an opportunity to ask for help.
- Whether removing a handoff shifts work or responsibility onto individuals and families.
- Whether a faster route excludes people who need additional support.

Use the map to understand the purpose of each step before deciding what to simplify.
