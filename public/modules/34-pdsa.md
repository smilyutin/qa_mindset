**Plan–Do–Study–Act: Test a Change, Learn and Decide**

---

## Definition

Plan–Do–Study–Act (PDSA) is a cycle for testing a change, comparing results with a prediction, and deciding what to do next.

Start with a small trial. Record what you expect to happen, study what actually happens, and use that learning to shape the next cycle.

---

## Relevant Example

> “I would test a revised intake checklist with one team. We would predict that it reduces incomplete submissions, run a small trial, review the results, and adopt, adapt, or abandon the change.”

| Stage | Apply it to the intake checklist |
| --- | --- |
| Plan | Identify why submissions are incomplete. Agree on a revised checklist, predict the improvement, and define the trial scope, duration and measures. |
| Do | Trial the checklist with one team. Record results, participant feedback and anything unexpected. |
| Study | Compare the results with the prediction and the previous process. Explore why results differed and whether the trial created other problems. |
| Act | Adopt the change for further testing, adapt it and run another cycle, or abandon it based on what was learned. |

A promising small trial provides learning for the next test; it does not establish that the change will work in every setting.

---

## Measures

Use the same definitions before and during the trial so the comparison is meaningful.

| Measure | What to record |
| --- | --- |
| Percentage of complete submissions | Complete submissions divided by all submissions received, multiplied by 100. Define what counts as complete. |
| Clarification calls per request | Number of clarification calls divided by the number of requests. |
| Staff time per submission | Time staff spend checking and processing each submission, including follow-up work. |

Record the number of submissions and relevant differences in request complexity. Review individual cases as well as averages to understand what changed.

## My Experience

> “My QA work involves identifying problems, validating changes, reviewing results, and checking for regressions. That provides a foundation for PDSA, but I would distinguish iterative testing from formally running documented PDSA cycles.”

The connection to QA is practical: define the problem, state the expected result, test a change, inspect the evidence and check for unintended effects. A documented PDSA cycle makes the prediction, observations, learning and next decision explicit.

---

## Service-User Involvement and Unintended Effects

> “I would ask participants whether the checklist is clear and accessible. I would also check whether it increases completion time or discourages people from applying.”

Invite people using the service to review the checklist and share feedback during the trial. Include people with different language, accessibility and support needs.

Alongside submission completeness, check:

- Whether participants understand the wording and know what information to provide.
- Whether the format works with their accessibility needs and available support.
- Whether completing the checklist takes longer or shifts work from staff to participants.
- Whether people abandon the process or decide against applying.

A higher percentage of complete submissions can be misleading if fewer people can complete the application. Study access and participant experience alongside efficiency.

---

## Record Each Cycle

Keep a short record of:

- The problem, proposed change and prediction.
- Who takes part, the trial dates and the measures.
- What happened, including unexpected effects and participant feedback.
- How the results compare with the prediction.
- The decision to adopt, adapt or abandon, and what to test next.
